Adaptive Lighting App

UX designAI trustFigma
View Live Prototype

The Problem

AI-driven wellness tools tend to ask for a lot of blind faith: the system tells you what to do, and you're expected to just trust it. For a biometric adaptive lighting concept — one that reads your body's signals and tells you to change your room's lighting — that trust gap is the whole product. In a 7-day hackathon sprint, the core UX question was how to make an automated recommendation feel earned rather than imposed.

Process

I designed and prototyped the full concept solo in 7 days, taking it from an idea to a working 4-screen clickable prototype: Home, Color Selection, Insights, and Smart Room.

Design Decisions

The Home screen surfaces the user's current biometric state — heart rate, HRV, stress — as a single glanceable "mood" indicator rather than a wall of raw numbers, so any recommendation that follows always starts from something the user can immediately relate to their own body.

Color Selection is where trust actually gets built. Instead of just prescribing "Soft Blue," a Current Analysis section names the specific signals behind the call — elevated stress, reduced recovery, high focus load — tied to context like a long study session, and a "Why this color?" explainer closes the loop before the user has to take anything on faith.

To keep that trust from eroding after the novelty wears off, Insights tracks each color's actual success rate against the goal it was meant to serve — calm, focus, recovery, energy — across day/week/month windows. That turns "trust me" into "here's the evidence, updated over time," which was the detail I cared about most: explainability shouldn't stop at the first interaction.

The last step, Smart Room, is where the recommendation becomes reality — syncing the chosen color across every connected fixture (LED strip, ceiling, floor lamp, desk lamp) with the same reasoning carried through, so the "why" never gets lost between screens.

Outcome

A complete, coherent 4-screen prototype built in 7 days, covering biometric sensing, explainable AI, historical validation, and smart-device control end-to-end.

What I'd Bring to a Team

Trust is the hardest thing to design for in any AI-driven product, and this project is where I learned to treat it as a first-class design requirement rather than an afterthought bolted on for compliance. That's the instinct I'd bring to a team building recommendation systems or anything else that asks users to act on something a machine decided: design the explanation before you design the feature, and keep proving the system is right long after the first interaction.
